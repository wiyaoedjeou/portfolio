# From Asperity Contact to Fluid Film: How Roughness Shifts the Lubrication Transition

When a rough contact is lubricated, the load is not carried in the same way at every speed. At low speed, it passes mainly through solid asperities. As speed increases, pressure generated in the fluid progressively helps separate the surfaces. Between these situations lies mixed lubrication, a regime that often controls friction, wear and safety.

The Stribeck curve is the classic summary of this evolution. Yet it can suggest that a single ratio between film thickness and roughness is enough to locate the transitions. A recent theoretical study by Vincent Bertin and Olivier Pouliquen offers a richer interpretation: speed, load and roughness are three independent controls, and the transition to hydrodynamic lubrication depends on both surface topography and applied load. [1]

This idea directly addresses a question left open in my doctoral research: how can a multi-asperity contact model be extended to include the hydrodynamic pressure generated by water or contaminants? [3] The connection is productive, provided that we distinguish what the new model demonstrates from what still needs to be validated for a real tyre-road contact.

## Why the classical Stribeck curve is not always enough

The Stribeck curve usually relates the coefficient of friction to a variable combining speed, viscosity and load. It distinguishes three broad domains:

- boundary lubrication, in which the surfaces remain largely in contact and friction strongly depends on asperity interactions;
- mixed lubrication, in which solid contact and fluid pressure carry the load simultaneously;
- hydrodynamic lubrication, in which a fluid film separates most of the surfaces and viscous losses become dominant.

This representation is extremely useful for organising the physics. On its own, however, it does not explain how the geometry of a rough surface changes local pressure distributions, mean separation or the fraction of the load carried by the fluid.

A common practice is to compare film thickness with a roughness amplitude. This ratio is a useful indicator, but it compresses surfaces with potentially different slopes, wavelengths, summit shapes and valley networks into one value. The same ratio does not therefore guarantee the same contact morphology.

The important result from Bertin and Pouliquen is precisely that the transition is not reduced to a constant ratio. Their minimal model produces a boundary that depends on roughness **and** load. [1]

## Who carries the load: asperities or fluid pressure?

The central question is not merely whether fluid is present. We need to determine how the total normal load is shared between two pressure networks:

1. contact pressure transmitted where asperities touch;
2. hydrodynamic pressure generated by flow through the gaps separating the surfaces.

At low speed, the fluid has more time to escape. Asperities therefore carry a large fraction of the load. As speed rises, the flow generates pressure that can support an increasing share of that load. Real solid contact area and summit pressures progressively decrease, although they do not necessarily vanish at once.

Mixed lubrication is therefore not a simple average between dry contact and complete separation. It is a coupled state: elastic deformation changes the space available to the fluid, while fluid pressure changes the deformation and contact of the asperities.

The recent model formalises this coupling through a homogenised pressure decomposition. At low speed, friction reduction is linked to the gradual transfer of load from asperities to hydrodynamic pressure. At high speed, friction retains both a viscous contribution and a residual contact contribution. [1]

## Three independent controls: speed, load and roughness

Dimensional analysis of the model identifies three independent parameters associated with speed, normal load and surface roughness. This conclusion sounds simple, but it changes how the transition should be interpreted.

Increasing speed promotes the build-up of hydrodynamic pressure. Changing the load changes elastic deformation, separation and the pressure required to support the contact. Changing roughness transforms both the passages available to the fluid and the population of asperities capable of transmitting load.

These effects are not interchangeable. Two tests giving the same coefficient of friction at one instant may have different load-sharing states. Likewise, a rougher surface does not systematically produce more or less friction: the effect depends on the regime, load, speed and the way roughness is defined.

| Regime | Main load carrier | Dominant contribution to friction | Particularly useful surface information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boundary | Solid asperities | Contact and interfacial shear | Summits, slopes and real contact area |
| Mixed | Asperities and fluid pressure | Residual contact and viscous dissipation | Height distribution, separation and flow paths |
| Hydrodynamic | Fluid film | Viscous shear | Film geometry, viscosity and speed |

This table remains a framework rather than a set of universal boundaries. The phase diagram proposed by the authors shows precisely how those boundaries move through a multidimensional parameter space. [1]

## Roughness is more than a thickness to overcome

In a lubricated contact, roughness plays at least two roles. It determines which asperities can locally carry load, but it also structures the volumes and connections through which fluid can flow.

An average amplitude such as `Ra` or `Sq` is therefore insufficient to describe the whole problem. Two surfaces with the same amplitude can have very different slopes, curvatures and spacing. Their real contacts and flow paths will not necessarily be equivalent.

This is where a multiscale interpretation becomes essential. Topography contains features of different sizes: larger scales contribute to global geometry and drainage, while finer scales control summits, local gaps and the possible rupture of a residual film. Persson’s contact theory, on which the new model builds, examines the interface at increasing magnification to relate statistical roughness to contact and separation. [1, 2]

This does not mean that wavelet decomposition and a mean-field contact theory are identical. They provide complementary viewpoints: the former locates topographic changes by scale; the latter seeks to convert a roughness spectrum into contact and separation quantities.

## A natural extension of my contact research

In my doctoral work, three-dimensional maps of pavements and aggregate mosaics are decomposed across multiple scales. A half-space contact model is then applied to those topographies to calculate contact pressures, rubber displacements and real contact area. Rubber viscoelasticity makes it possible to estimate a friction component associated with its deformation. [3]

That framework answers the question: how do texture, polishing and rubber properties change solid contact and viscoelastic dissipation? It does not explicitly solve the flow of water through the interface. The thesis itself identifies the addition of shear, hydrodynamic pressure due to water or contaminants, and local contact temperature as future extensions. [3]

The work by Bertin and Pouliquen supplies a theoretical building block that speaks directly to this perspective. It describes load redistribution between a rough elastic contact and a Newtonian fluid. It does not replace the model developed in the thesis: it addresses a different coupling under different assumptions. But the two approaches share key variables—topography, pressure, separation, contact area and load.

A coherent research route would therefore retain a multiscale description of real surfaces, then jointly calculate the viscoelastic response of rubber and fluid pressure. The goal would no longer be to estimate dry contact or drainage separately, but to track how much load each carries through the transition.

## What transfers to wet pavement — and what does not

Tyre-road contact in rain makes this issue immediately concrete. Pavement macrotexture helps evacuate water, tyre grooves redistribute it, and microtexture contributes to contact where a discontinuous residual film remains. Hydrodynamic pressure can locally lift the rubber and reduce the area of solid contact. [3]

The new model supports three qualitative ideas relevant to this problem:

- solid contact and fluid pressure can carry the load simultaneously;
- the transition depends on speed, load and roughness;
- hydrodynamic separation is not necessarily defined by a constant geometric ratio.

However, its phase diagram should not be presented as a law already validated for tyres. The model considers a Newtonian fluid, rough elastic contact and a homogenised description. A real tyre adds viscoelasticity, tread patterns, evolving geometry, transient inlet and outlet conditions, non-uniform water depths, temperature, slip and sometimes contaminants.

The transfer is therefore a research hypothesis, not an experimental conclusion. It helps formulate better tests, but it cannot yet predict braking distance or hydroplaning speed from topography alone.

## How the transition could be tested experimentally

To confront this theoretical framework with pavement surfaces, the three controls would need to be varied without discarding surface information.

A relevant protocol could combine:

1. 3D topography measured before and after polishing, with documented resolution and measurement area;
2. multiscale descriptors of heights, slopes, curvatures, summit volumes and drainage paths;
3. several normal loads and sliding speeds;
4. controlled water depth, viscosity and temperature;
5. simultaneous friction measurement and, if possible, an indicator of separation or real contact area;
6. comparisons between surfaces with similar average amplitude but different spatial organisation.

The decisive point would be to identify not only a reduction in friction, but also the associated mechanism: reduced solid area, increasing fluid pressure, changing viscoelastic dissipation, or a combination of these effects.

This strategy would avoid treating two surfaces as equivalent simply because they have the same `Ra`, or two regimes as equivalent simply because they momentarily produce the same friction.

## Towards an operating map rather than a single threshold

For engineering, the value of a phase diagram is that it replaces a single boundary with an operating map. A surface could be positioned according to its texture, load, speed and fluid properties, then tracked as polishing progressively modifies its asperities.

Such a representation would help compare materials, design a test programme and identify the conditions under which a dry-contact model ceases to be sufficient. It would also connect several topics already discussed here: multiscale characterisation, contact mechanics, the limits of `Ra`, and wet-road sensing.

The next step is therefore not to search for another universal indicator. It is to build a testable model in which topography, solid and fluid remain identifiable, then determine which scales are actually active in each regime.

## What to remember

The lubrication transition is not controlled by speed alone. It emerges from load sharing between asperities and fluid, itself influenced by load and roughness.

The new model from Bertin and Pouliquen generalises the classical Stribeck interpretation by proposing a regime space governed by three independent parameters. It offers a promising framework for rough lubricated contacts and directly illuminates a perspective formulated in my thesis.

For wet pavements, the connection is scientifically relevant but still requires experimental demonstration. The value of a multiscale approach will be to determine which topographic components carry contact, organise flow and shift the transitions.
