Boundary Element Method for Rough Contact Problems: A Practical Introduction

By Wiyao Edjeou, PhD — Tribology & Contact Mechanics Engineer  ·  wiyaoedjeou@outlook.com

Finite element models get most of the attention in contact mechanics, but for rough surface contact — the kind you actually encounter in wheel-rail interaction or tire-road friction — the Boundary Element Method (BEM) is often the more practical tool. It avoids meshing the bulk of the solid, scales far better with surface resolution, and handles the multiscale nature of real roughness naturally. Here's a practical walkthrough based on work done on rail surface roughness and pavement texture.

1. Why BEM fits rough contact so well

Rough surface contact spans length scales from millimeters down to microns. A full 3D FEM mesh fine enough to capture micro-asperities is often computationally prohibitive. BEM sidesteps this:

  • Only the contacting surface needs to be discretized, not the full volume.
  • The elastic half-space assumption (valid when contact dimensions are small relative to body size) lets you use fast, well-established Green's function solutions.
  • FFT-accelerated algorithms make it feasible to run full-resolution roughness maps — hundreds of thousands of surface points — in seconds rather than hours.

This is exactly the kind of problem you meet when trying to explain how surface texture at different scales affects skid resistance or wheel-rail friction: you need enough resolution to resolve the small scales without paying an FEM-level computational cost.

2. Setting up the model

Surface input

Real measured roughness data (from profilometry or laser scanning) is the ideal input. Represent the surface as a height map on a regular grid — this is what most BEM contact-solving algorithms (Polonsky-Keer type, or the classic Kalker approach for rolling contact) expect.

Elastic properties

For a half-space formulation, only the effective elastic modulus and Poisson's ratio of the contacting pair matter — no need to define full 3D elastic geometry. This is one of the biggest practical wins over FEM: material definition takes minutes, not hours.

Solving the normal contact problem

The core algorithm iterates on the pressure distribution until the gap function satisfies the contact constraints (non-negative pressure, non-negative gap, and the correct total load). FFT-based conjugate gradient methods are the standard choice — they scale as O(N log N) instead of O(N²) for the elastic influence coefficient calculations, which is what makes high-resolution roughness maps tractable at all.

3. Common pitfalls

  • Periodic boundary artifacts — FFT-based solvers assume periodicity. If your measured roughness patch isn't representative or large enough, edge effects distort the pressure field. Pad or window the data appropriately.
  • Resolution vs. domain size trade-off — capturing both the finest asperities and a representative macroscopic patch requires balancing grid resolution against computational cost. Multiscale decomposition becomes valuable rather than optional.
  • Elastic half-space assumption breakdown — for thin coatings or layered materials (common in rail or pavement applications), the pure half-space assumption doesn't hold, and a layered-medium Green's function is needed instead.

This kind of rough contact model is the foundation for predicting real contact area, friction coefficient evolution with polishing, or water film height effects on skid resistance — problems that come up constantly in tire-road and wheel-rail tribology.

4. Where this leads

It also connects naturally to fatigue and wear prediction, since real (not nominal) contact pressure is what actually drives subsurface stress and crack initiation.
