Multiscale Surface Roughness and Skid Resistance: Why Scale Matters

By Wiyao Edjeou, PhD — Tribology & Contact Mechanics Engineer · wiyaoedjeou@outlook.com

26 August 2026 · 5 min read

Ask a road engineer what makes a pavement “grippy” and you will usually hear about macrotexture — the visible aggregate structure you can feel underfoot. That is only half the picture. Skid resistance, especially in wet conditions, is governed by roughness across several decades of length scale at once. Treating it as a single-scale property hides the mechanisms that control friction evolution. This is the central idea behind the multiscale texture work developed during my PhD and later applied at EASE.

1. Two regimes, two mechanisms

Tire-road friction in wet conditions involves contributions whose relative importance varies with texture scale:

  • Macrotexture, roughly 0.5–50 mm — supports water drainage and affects the tire deformation mechanisms involved at higher speeds.
  • Microtexture, below roughly 0.5 mm — contributes to the local interaction between rubber and mineral asperities, particularly at lower speeds.

A pavement can retain strong macrotexture and still become slippery as its finer texture is polished by traffic. A single texture metric can therefore signal that a surface changed without identifying the scale — and mechanism — responsible.

2. Measuring across scales

Instrumentation trade-offs

No single instrument captures the full relevant range equally well. Laser profilometry covers larger texture wavelengths, while focus-variation microscopy resolves finer features over smaller patches. A robust multiscale protocol must connect these observations without confusing instrument bandwidth with physical surface change.

Wavelet decomposition

Instead of reducing the surface to one roughness parameter, continuous wavelet analysis decomposes its profile into scale bands. Tracking each band during polishing reveals which wavelengths contribute to the initial friction gain and which govern the longer-term decline.

3. From texture to friction

Connecting a characterized surface to friction requires more than a texture statistic. Depending on the question, the modelling chain may combine:

  • A contact model — often BEM-based; see the companion article on rough contact — to estimate real contact area and local pressure.
  • A viscoelastic rubber description to account for speed-, frequency-, and temperature-dependent dissipation.
  • A description of water evacuation when the operating condition and texture range make drainage relevant.

An embedded estimation approach — inferring water-film height and skid resistance from in-vehicle sensor data rather than dedicated survey vehicles — was explored for autonomous-shuttle experimentation in the ENA project and assessed through instrumented braking tests.

4. Practical implications

For infrastructure managers, a scale-resolved diagnosis can make a friction measurement actionable. Different mechanisms alter coarse and fine texture at different rates, so identifying the affected wavelengths helps distinguish polishing from other forms of surface deterioration and supports a better-targeted maintenance response.
